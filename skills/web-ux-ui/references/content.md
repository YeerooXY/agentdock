# Concrete wording and evidence

Write from verified product facts and the visitor's next decision. Use concrete names, actions and consequences. Preserve prices, units, eligibility, limitations, privacy information and recovery instructions.

Replace interchangeable promises with the actual behavior:

| Weak draft | Useful direction |
| --- | --- |
| Unlock your potential | Name what the person can do with this product |
| A seamless workflow | Describe which steps or records are connected |
| More than a platform | State the product category and specific job |
| Get started | Name the real next step: Download for Windows, Check availability, Create project |
| Something went wrong | Explain the known failure and a usable retry or correction |

Do not infer AI authorship from a phrase. Fix the content problem: vagueness, missing facts, repetition or misleading action labels. Do not replace one generic slogan with another. When the project lists prohibited wording, report each occurrence with its location and an example; fix within scope and flag unrelated occurrences.

Remove small explanatory text that repeats a heading or an obvious control. Remove repeated workflow summaries when the product demonstration already communicates them. Use plain platform names such as Windows and Android; add a requirement only where it changes the decision. Keep prices, limitations and recovery steps visible. Put screenshot provenance or secondary explanation in an appropriate detail view instead of repeating a caption beneath every screen.

Avoid slogan-like sequences of short commands, especially rhetorical triplets such as "Read your consoles. Send a message. Open your files." Use an ordinary sentence describing the relevant behavior, for example: "You can check a running session and reply from your phone." Keep the tone natural and factual; remove clever-sounding transitions, exaggerated confidence and decorative rhythm. This does not prohibit useful factual lists or necessary sequential instructions.

## Page copy workflow

1. Establish the real offer, audience, primary action and constraints from the project. Mark missing proof instead of inventing it.
2. Draft headings that describe each section's job. Remove sections that add no decision-relevant information.
3. Put consequences near the action: cost, recurring terms, platform availability, delivery conditions, what is submitted or downloaded.
4. Use links for destinations and buttons for actions. A CTA opening a selection step should describe that step. Don't label a lead form as an instant download.
5. Read every sentence over 20 words for unnecessary clauses. Check short copy for contrast reveals, self-answered promotional questions, decorative uppercase labels and repeated reassurances.
6. Test the actual wording in narrow layouts and error states. Keep meaningful labels visible; don't rely on placeholder text alone.

Never invent testimonials, customers, usage numbers, guarantees, certifications or conversion results. Clearly identify illustrative examples and keep them separate from operational status or live social proof. Use competitor content to study specificity and information sequence, not to copy prose or treat its performance as known.

Sources: [GOV.UK writing guidance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk), [Apple writing guidance](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing), [NN/G usability of concise scannable text](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/). Apply the user's voice and current project canon first.
